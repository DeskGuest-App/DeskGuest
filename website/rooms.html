Rooms & live collaboration

Fixing a machine? Start a session. Gathering people? Host a room. In DeskGuest the collaboration is native to the session itself — and a room is simply the place where a group's sessions, people, and history live.

A session is already a meeting

There is no meeting object to create, no mode to switch into, nothing to schedule before people can work together. From the moment a session starts, it natively carries everything a working session needs.

Live view, voice, and chat

The shared screen, a voice channel, and text chat are part of every session from the first second — for the person helping, the person being helped, and anyone else taking part. Cameras are off by default; turning one on is each participant's own act.

Several participants, each one named

People and AI agents can take part in the same session, each under their own name, face, and permissions, each named in the roster and in the presence layer. An AI can join just to watch, take notes, and point things out — without driving.

Pointing, annotating, noting

Any participant can point at what they mean — point-and-hold annotations on the shared screen — and the session feed narrates each participant's actions as they happen. Shared session notes gather what the group is working out, in the session where it's being worked out.

Guests arrive by invite

Someone outside the group joins the same way one-time help does: an expiring, single-use invite link — in the browser for view, voice, and chat, or one tap further for the full session. How joining works

Working side by side

When two people need the wheel at once, DeskGuest doesn't make them take turns by passing a single pointer back and forth.

Floor mode — the default

One driver at a time. Anyone else can ask for the floor, and the handover is explicit: the presence name changes, the record notes it, and the person at the device watches it happen.

Side-by-side — chosen in the open

Two participants drive simultaneously, each with their own named, coloured ghost pointer. Keyboard focus follows a simple rule — the last click, or an explicit grab — and every input is attributed to its participant in the record. Side-by-side is a visible choice, never a quiet escalation.

Watching is a real role

A second operator can shadow a live session — watching, recorded as a watcher, while presence names the driver. Shadowing is how a senior tech supervises, a trainee learns, or a second pair of eyes checks the work.

One STOP stops everyone. However many participants a session has — drivers, watchers, agents — the device owner's STOP ends the whole session at once. Presence names every participant for the entire session; there is no audience invisible to the person being helped.

Rooms: the standing place

A session is the live event; a room is the place it happens in. Rooms are optional — collaboration never requires one — and they're for the work that recurs: the family computer, the project team, the customer site.

Owned by a group, with named members

A room belongs to a group and has named members — the people (and agents) who belong there. One standing share link brings members in; the room's membership is the group's to govern, under the group's admins.

A memory of everything that happened

Session records, recordings, notes, and receipts are filed in the room where they happened. Months later, "what did we do about that printer?" has an answer with a record attached — not a chat scrollback.

Sessions, your way

A session can be standalone, scheduled standalone, started inside a room, or moved into one mid-flight. And a device pulled into a room session stays under its own owner's law: that device's presence, approvals, and STOP are never diluted by the room it was pulled into.

A receipt for the person helped

After every session — in a room or out of one — a plain-language summary is left on the helped device: who was here, what they did, in that device's language. Help leaves a paper trail the helped person can actually read.
